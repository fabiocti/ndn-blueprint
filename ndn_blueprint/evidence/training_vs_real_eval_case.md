# Case Study: Training Metrics vs Real-World Evaluation

## Summary

Good training metrics do not guarantee good real-world performance. AOJ v3 is the documented example: every internal metric improved, yet real A/B performance regressed by 7 percentage points. This case study explains what happened, why it matters, and how to avoid repeating the mistake.

---

## The AOJ v3 Experiment

### Context
AOJ v2 achieved 73% fact recovery on the real OpenClaw A/B test. The remaining misses were taxonomized: 58% exact numeric counts, 25% OOV domain names, 17% error/status strings.

### Hypothesis
Stronger token-level loss weighting for entity and digit tokens would improve preservation of these specific token types and lift real-world fact recovery.

### Interventions
- **Token-ID entity weighting**: encode known entity strings through the tokenizer, collect all sub-word token IDs (122 tokens identified), apply 3.0x loss weight
- **Stronger digit weight**: increased from 3.0x to 5.0x
- **Expanded SLD vocabulary**: added the specific OOV targets from the miss analysis (bostonacoustics, xvtest, jbl, etc.)

---

## The Divergence

### Internal metrics: v3 was better across the board

| Metric | v2 (ep10) | v3 (ep10) | v3 vs v2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| val_loss | 0.0016 | 0.0001 | **16x better** |
| exact_match onset | Epoch 9 | Epoch 6 | **3 epochs earlier** |
| shuffled_gap | 4.31 | 4.37 | Better |
| numeric_token_acc | 100% | 100% | Same |

By every internal measure, v3 was the superior model.

### Real A/B: v3 was worse

| Metric | v2 | v3 | Delta |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fact recovery | 73% | 66% | **-7pp** |
| S2 facts | 60% | 53% | **-7pp** |
| S4 facts | 75% | 60% | **-15pp** |
| Compression | 1.69x | 1.66x | ~same |
| Continuity | 0.92 | 0.92 | same |

The regression was concentrated in the two most fact-dense slices (S2 and S4).

---

## Why The Divergence Happened

### The synthetic eval was within-distribution
The held-out evaluation used synthetic data generated by the same corpus generator (different seed). v3's stronger loss weighting helped it reproduce entity tokens more accurately within this distribution. Internal metrics correctly reflected that v3 was better at reproducing synthetic journal patterns.

### The real A/B was out-of-distribution
Real OpenClaw journals have entity distributions, fact densities, and contextual patterns that the synthetic generator only approximates. The loss weighting that helped on synthetic eval distorted the model's behavior on real data.

### Over-optimization on entity tokens hurt contextual recall
The 3.0x entity weight and 5.0x digit weight made the model allocate disproportionate capacity to reproducing individual entity and digit tokens. This came at the expense of surrounding contextual tokens that carry factual meaning. The model got "sharper" on isolated tokens but lost coherence on fact-dense passages.

### The loss landscape shifted
Upweighting specific token categories changes the loss surface. The optimization found a different local minimum — one that is better at synthetic entity reproduction but worse at holistic fact recovery on real data.

---

## The Lesson

### Internal metrics are necessary but not sufficient
Training metrics tell you whether the model is converging and whether the latent space is learning structure. They do not tell you whether the model will recover specific facts from real-world text.

### Synthetic eval can mislead
Held-out evaluation on synthetic data tests generalization within the synthetic distribution. It does not test generalization to the real world. The gap between synthetic and real performance is a real and documented phenomenon.

### "Better on every metric" can still be worse
v3 was better on val_loss (16x), exact_match onset (3 epochs earlier), and shuffled_gap. It was worse on the one metric that matters: real-world fact recovery. Trusting the aggregate of internal metrics would have led to the wrong conclusion.

### Loss-level interventions have limits
Token-level loss weighting is a blunt instrument. It optimizes for individual token reproduction, not for holistic factual coherence. For the entity preservation problem, architectural changes (copy/pointer mechanisms, entity-aware attention) are likely needed.

---

## How to Avoid This in the Future

1. **Always run real-world evaluation.** Internal metrics alone are never the final answer. Any change that claims to improve real-world performance must be validated on real data.

2. **Use the same real-world test.** Keep the evaluation constant across versions (same slices, same facts, same scoring). Moving goalposts make it impossible to detect regressions.

3. **Be suspicious of "better on every metric."** When everything improves, ask: is the evaluation testing what actually matters? Improvement on a proxy metric does not guarantee improvement on the target metric.

4. **Document negative results.** AOJ v3 is a failed experiment, and documenting it prevents repeating the same mistake. The next intervention can target a different lever.

5. **Separate the levers.** v3 changed three things simultaneously (entity weight, digit weight, SLD vocabulary). It is not clear which change caused the regression. Future experiments should change one thing at a time when possible.

## Postscript: AOJ v4 Further Validates This Finding

### What happened
AOJ v4 was trained on a fundamentally different data mix: 50% real GitHub operational journals + 45% synthetic + 5% OpenClaw real data (v2 was 100% synthetic). On the same 5-slice OpenClaw A/B test with the same scoring harness, v4 achieved 99% fact recovery at 1.78x compression — up from v2's 73%.

### The internal metrics went the "wrong" direction
v4's val_loss is 0.0020, higher than v2's 0.0014. v4's shuffled_gap is 14.41, much higher than v2's 4.25. By naive internal-metric comparison, v4 looks like a mixed bag: worse val_loss, much better latent dependence. Yet its real-world performance jumped +26pp.

### Why this matters for this case study
v3 showed that better internal metrics can mean worse real-world performance (all metrics improved, real A/B regressed). v4 shows the converse: higher val_loss can coexist with dramatically better real-world performance. The common thread is that internal metrics measure fit to training distribution, not generalization to real data. v4's real-data training mix brought the training distribution closer to the test distribution, which is what actually mattered.

### The 73% ceiling was a data problem
v2 and v3 both trained on 100% synthetic data. No amount of loss engineering (v3) could overcome the distribution mismatch between synthetic training data and real agent journals. v4 solved this by changing the training data itself. This is the strongest evidence yet that training metrics vs real-world performance divergence is fundamentally a data distribution problem.

### Data leakage caveat
The 5 OpenClaw test journals were in v4's training set (5% real data mix). This is the same caveat as v2. The 99% result should be interpreted with this in mind — the test is not fully held-out.

### TDR scale benchmark
v4 on TDR scale: 17/20 hits (85%), 91% fact recovery, 86x compression. v2 on the same test: 18/20 (90%), 93% fact recovery, 89x compression. v4 is slightly worse on retrieval-heavy evaluation despite being much better on single-session compression. This further demonstrates that single-session compression quality (where v4 excels) and scale retrieval quality (where the pipeline matters more than the model) are distinct problems.
